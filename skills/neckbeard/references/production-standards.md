# Learning production standards

## Shared direction
Create a clean, seamless, natural learning experience: minimalist but never sterile. Combine restrained elegance, fluid and purposeful interaction, and practical product understanding. Warmth comes from considerate explanation, meaningful examples, and graceful recovery. Precision takes precedence over polish. Follow explicit project requirements; otherwise apply these defaults. Branding and aesthetic choices must preserve the destination project's identity.

## Video
Use considered materials, precise proportions, generous negative space, purposeful typography, and restrained depth. Keep one primary focus per shot. Use simple editorial metaphors connected to the explanation: layers reveal complexity, alignment creates clarity, and convergence expresses connection.

Use smooth spring-based motion with believable weight, subtle overshoot where appropriate, and gentle settling. Selection, proximity, focus, or implied touch may prompt precise micro-interactions. Every movement must clarify a relationship, guide attention, or reveal operation. Tune motion to the element and task; avoid a universal spring preset.

Balance fluid transitions with stillness. Preserve spatial and conceptual continuity between scenes. Hold consequential states long enough to read and understand. Avoid excessive bounce, flashy effects, clutter, arbitrary movement, and constant zoom. Keep actual controls and product feedback truthful; distinguish editorial overlays from application behavior. Never animate a planned feature as though it exists.

For each shot record learning purpose, verified action, visible state, narration, annotation, motion purpose, continuity, hold, and evidence. Default delivery proposal: 1080p, 16:9, 30 fps MP4; adapt to device and brief. These are production defaults, not promises of tool support.

## Audio
Create a calm, natural, quietly expressive voice: assured without sounding promotional, warm without theatricality. Speak as a capable colleague helping someone perform a task. Use conversational phrasing and contractions where appropriate. Avoid exaggerated enthusiasm, robotic cadence, breathless delivery, and filler introductions.

Use emphasis to mark decisions, changes, and consequences. Give each sentence one main idea. Vary cadence gently; allow silence after important actions and unfamiliar concepts. Start around 135–155 words per minute for straightforward narration, slowing for technical terms and new decisions. Measure the actual render and adjust to comprehension rather than forcing a target duration. Never time-compress speech merely to fit visuals.

Match narration to what is visible. Identify a control before the action, explain the consequence as the result appears, then leave reading time. Avoid simultaneously reading dense screen text verbatim. For standalone audio include navigation context, exact labels, checkpoints, and explicit opportunities to pause; do not rely on 'here', visual position, or colour. Produce a separate audio adaptation where needed rather than automatically extracting a video track.

Maintain a pronunciation dictionary for names, acronyms, commands, and domain terms. Preserve exact written commands in the transcript; supply spoken forms separately. Choose language, accent, voice, and pronunciation from the brief, without inferring identity. Use a consistent voice across a series.

Keep sound clean, close, and consistent. Avoid distracting room sound, clipping, pumping, and overprocessing. Music is off by default; use subtle effects only to clarify meaningful events. Proposed web mix targets: approximately -16 LUFS integrated for stereo and true peak no higher than -1 dBTP. Treat these as house targets, measure with available tools, and adapt to the delivery platform. If measurement is unavailable, mark mix compliance unverified. Keep an uncompressed WAV master where supported and provide a requested listening format. Review the actual audio for pronunciation, missing words, artifacts, pacing, and sync; script approval alone does not establish audio quality.

## Technical documents
Make documents feel considered, readable, and useful at the moment of work. Use a clear title, concise purpose, visible hierarchy, generous spacing, restrained colour, consistent typography, and stable placement of navigation and metadata. Avoid ornamental cover pages for short task guides, dense walls of text, gratuitous callouts, and excessive nesting.

Organize around reader goals. Begin with the outcome and prerequisites; place concepts where they enable the next decision. Use progressive disclosure: a concise main path with optional explanation and nearby reference. Keep procedures, conceptual explanations, and reference information distinct but connected. Give each section one job.

Write direct, precise sentences with familiar terms. Define necessary jargon once. Preserve exact interface labels, identifiers, command syntax, units, defaults, and constraints. Explain consequential choices and tradeoffs without adding implementation detail that does not help the audience. Distinguish required, optional, and conditional steps.

Make examples copyable and realistic. Mark placeholders and illustrative outputs. Verify commands and configuration against the inspected version. Place warnings before consequential steps and recovery guidance near the likely failure. Use tables for exact mappings and comparisons; diagrams for relationships that prose obscures. Include accessible text alternatives, meaningful links, semantic headings, and selectable text. Never encode meaning only through colour.

For rendered documents inspect actual pages for clipping, awkward breaks, unreadable tables, orphan headings, and code wrapping. Keep source revision, evidence, limitations, and maintenance details in a companion record so learner guidance stays clear. Do not claim a document is accessible solely from its appearance.

## Tutorials and learning paths
Design a deliberate rhythm: orient, demonstrate, practise, confirm, then transfer. Start with one meaningful outcome and a realistic task. Establish only essential prerequisites. Demonstrate one action or coherent action group at a time, explain its consequence, and show the resulting state. Use actual product language and realistic sanitized data.

Give each procedure a goal, starting state, role/access requirements, numbered actions, expected feedback, completion criteria, and recovery or reset path. Introduce optional depth after the first useful result. Fade support from worked example to guided practice to independent task when the scope warrants it. Keep practice observable; quizzes cannot replace task performance.

Anticipate uncertainty without clutter: explain what is saved, visible to others, reversible, or final when evidence supports it. Show common mistakes and recovery at the point they matter. Keep end-user, administrator, and contributor needs separate. A coherent experience across formats preserves the same labels, sequence, examples, outcome, and evidence while adapting explanations to the medium.

## Scripts and storyboards
Write for speech rather than reading a technical document aloud. Use active verbs and short, connected sentences. Separate narration, on-screen copy, product actions, and production directions. Each segment must advance the learning goal. Avoid slogan openings, decorative metaphors, and unsupported benefit claims.

For every shot specify: ID, approximate duration, starting state, action, resulting state, narration, on-screen copy, motion and purpose, hold, audio cue, accessibility note, and evidence reference. Timing remains estimated until media exists. Resolve pronunciation and terminology before synthesis. Build caption text from the final spoken rendition; validate timing after edits. Provide a transcript with important visual context, plus descriptions of essential visual information where narration does not cover it.

## Acceptance and handoff
Review correctness, instructional usefulness, accessibility, visual restraint, audio intelligibility, and cross-format consistency separately. Record pass/fail/unverified with concrete evidence and needed correction. Inspect the finished artifact, not only its source. Do not claim learner effectiveness without learner evidence.

Retain editable scripts, storyboard, production brief, caption source, revision identifiers, and a dependency map from steps/shots to product features. When a feature changes, flag affected assets for review; do not claim automatic re-recording exists. No media tools means a production-ready package only, clearly identified as such.
